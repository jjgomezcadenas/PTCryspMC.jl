\begin{abstract}
\noindent
\texttt{PTCryspMC.jl} produces, for a given PET scanner, the list of coincidences it would record.
The detector response is computed by the engine of the companion document \texttt{PTCryspMC\_phys};
this document describes how that engine is driven. The simulator addresses two operation scenarios
that share the engine and differ only in the source --- where the annihilations are, and how many
there are. In the \emph{clinical} scenario a radiotracer of known activity is distributed in a
phantom or a patient; in the \emph{after-proton-irradiation} (API) scenario the source is the
positron activity that a proton dose leaves behind. The engine and the coincidence chain are
complete and validated on a controlled water phantom --- the simplest clinical source; the
activity-driven clinical front-end and the API scenario reader are described here as the design being
built on that validated base.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The purpose of the simulator is to generate, for each candidate scanner, the list-mode file of lines
of response (LORs) that a measurement would yield. That list is the input to a separate analysis ---
recovering the proton range, or comparing detectors --- which is independent of the simulation and
described elsewhere.

The engine --- the physics, geometry, transport, and detector model --- is the same in both
scenarios. What changes is the \emph{source}: the spatial distribution of the annihilation points and
how their number and timing are fixed.

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=1.5em]
\item[Clinical scenario.] A radiotracer of a single isotope --- fluorine-18 by default --- is
distributed in the field of view at a known activity. The activity sets both how many annihilations
occur, through the number of decays in the acquisition window, and when each occurs, through the
decay curve. This is conventional PET; its controlled limit, a uniform water phantom, is where the
engine is validated.
\item[After-proton-irradiation (API) scenario.] The annihilations are drawn from a \emph{scenario}:
the positron activity a proton field leaves, computed upstream and frozen. Here the number of decays
per isotope is measured, not derived --- it is read from the scenario --- and the spatial source is
the map of emitters the irradiation produced. This is the measurement the simulator ultimately
serves.
\end{description}

The contrast between them is clean: the clinical scenario is \emph{activity-driven} (the count
follows from the activity), the API scenario is \emph{count-driven} (the count is given). Everything
downstream of the source is identical, and both produce the same deliverable: a list-mode file in
which every LOR carries its two hit positions, energies and times, and a flag marking it a
\emph{true}, \emph{scatter}, or \emph{random} coincidence.

\section{The clinical scenario}
\label{sec:clinical}

\subsection{The source: a distribution and an activity}

Conventional PET images a radiotracer, so the source is two things: a spatial distribution --- where
the tracer is --- and an activity --- how much, which fixes both how many annihilations occur and
when.

\paragraph{The distribution.} The distribution is built from one or more \emph{regions}, each a solid
of the geometry filled to an activity concentration in \si{Bq/mL}. A single region --- a water sphere
or cylinder filled uniformly --- is the controlled phantom on which the engine is validated. Several
regions at different concentrations describe a structured source: a Derenzo phantom, or a NEMA
image-quality phantom with hot inserts against a warm background (a $4{:}1$ or $8{:}1$ ratio). An
annihilation point is drawn by choosing a region in proportion to its activity --- concentration
times volume --- and then a point uniformly within it. Activity concentration is the natural unit
here: it is how phantoms are filled and how images are quantified, and, unlike a single total, it
specifies a non-uniform source completely. The filled solid is also the attenuator, so a water source
produces realistic patient scatter.

\paragraph{From activity to events.} The tracer is one isotope, which carries a half-life $T_{1/2}$
and a positron branching fraction $\beta^+$ (fluorine-18: $T_{1/2}=\SI{110}{min}$, $\beta^+=0.967$).
Over the acquisition window $[t_0,t_1]$ of length $T$ the activity decays as
$A(t)=A_0\,e^{-\lambda(t-t_0)}$ with $\lambda=\ln 2/T_{1/2}$, and two numbers follow from it. The
number of nuclear decays in the window is the integral
\begin{equation}
  D \;=\; \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\! A(t)\,\mathrm{d}t \;=\; \frac{A_0}{\lambda}\,\bigl(1-e^{-\lambda T}\bigr),
  \label{eq:decays}
\end{equation}
where $A_0=\sum_i c_i V_i$ sums concentration times volume over the regions. Because only a fraction
$\beta^+$ of decays emit a positron, the number of annihilations the engine simulates is
$N = \beta^+ D$. Each annihilation's time is then drawn from the same decaying curve --- a truncated
exponential on $[t_0,t_1]$ --- front-loaded toward the start of the acquisition
(Fig.~\ref{fig:activity}). So the activity does double duty: it sets the count and it sets the
timing. For fluorine-18 over a typical scan the decay is mild (about $6\%$ over ten minutes), so the
activity is nearly constant and the times nearly uniform; for a short-lived isotope the front-loading
dominates.

The time of each event is computed deterministically from its event number, so it is the same
however the events were distributed across threads, and identical in every pass that reads it. This
per-event time is what the randoms pass needs.

\subsection{The run configuration}

A run is specified by a single TOML file with sections for the geometry, the source, the transport,
the detector, and the timing. The source section selects the scenario, names the isotope and the
acquisition window, and lists the regions, each a geometry volume and its concentration. The file
name is the run \emph{tag}, which names the output directory and is stamped into every output, so a
run carries its own provenance. Naming the crystal in the transport section pulls all of that
crystal's properties --- cross sections, light yield, scintillation decay, resolution, efficiency ---
from the materials database.

The controlled phantom on which the engine is validated is the limiting case of this configuration:
one region, and the count pinned directly rather than derived from an activity, so that a validation
run reproduces exactly. The activity-driven path of Eq.~\eqref{eq:decays} --- concentration,
branching, derived $N$ --- is the front-end that generalises that draw to a realistic clinical
source.

\subsection{The production chain}

The measurement is built in stages, each a streaming pass that scales to the large event counts a run
contains (Table~\ref{tab:chain}).

\paragraph{Singles.} \texttt{simulate\_source\_mt} draws the annihilations and transports their
photons, writing one row per detected photon: its first-interaction point and block, its summed
energy, and its time $t_{\mathrm{rel}}$ relative to the decay --- the time of flight plus the
scintillation jitter --- stamped once, here, so that every later stage reuses the same per-photon
time. The pass is multi-threaded over contiguous blocks of events; the chunking is fixed by the
configuration, so the singles are reproducible bit for bit and can be regenerated exactly from the
run's seed.

\paragraph{True coincidences.} \texttt{build\_true\_coincidences\_from\_singles} pairs the two photons
of each annihilation that both reached the ring contained in one block. Each pair becomes a LOR
carrying the two timestamps $t_1, t_2$, the residual
$\mathrm{d}t = (t_1 - t_2) - \mathrm{TOF_{diff}}$ --- in which the geometric time-of-flight difference
is removed, so $\mathrm{d}t$ measures the timing resolution alone --- and the per-photon
phantom-scatter counts, which separate a single scatter from a multiple one. These are the true and
scatter coincidences.

\paragraph{The coincidence window.} Two photons are a coincidence when their arrival times fall within
a window $\tau$. The window is read off the measured distribution of $\Delta T = |t_1 - t_2|$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:dt}): a few-nanosecond window already keeps almost every genuine pair. The chosen
$\tau = \SI{3}{ns}$ keeps about $99\%$ of the coincidences. Because $\Delta T$ is set mainly by the
spread in time of flight across the ring --- geometry, not the crystal --- the same window serves both
CsI and BGO.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{dt}
  \caption{Choosing the coincidence window. Left, the distribution of the coincidence-time difference
  $\Delta T = |t_1 - t_2|$. Right, the fraction of coincidences kept as the window $\tau$ is widened.
  The window $\tau = \SI{3}{ns}$ (dashed) keeps about $99\%$ of the pairs.}
  \label{fig:dt}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Randoms.} \texttt{build\_randoms\_from\_singles} forms the accidental background. It pairs
singles from \emph{different} annihilations whose absolute arrival times --- the decay time plus
$t_{\mathrm{rel}}$ --- fall within $\tau$. Because two unrelated decays must coincide to within a few
nanoseconds, randoms are rare and front-loaded: their rate is approximately $2\tau S^2$ in the singles
rate $S$, so it follows the square of the activity and fades faster than the true rate. In the
clinical scenario, where $S$ comes from a real activity, this random rate is physical.

\paragraph{Reconstructed coincidences.} \texttt{reco\_lors} merges the trues and the randoms into the
detector-level measurement. It applies the detector response (position and energy smearing), the
energy selection, and the coincidence-window cut $|t_1 - t_2| \le \tau$, and flags every surviving LOR
as true, scatter, or random. The energy selection is a lower cut on the smeared energy
(Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum}). Detector studies keep this cut low (\SI{300}{keV}) so the Compton shoulder
remains visible in the spectrum; the reconstruction raises it to the photopeak region (\SI{450}{keV}),
which selects full-energy events, suppresses scatter, and shrinks the file.

\subsection{The list-mode output}

The deliverable is \texttt{lors\_det.h5}: one record per coincidence, carrying the two hit positions,
the two energies, the two times, the per-gamma phantom-scatter counts, the timing residual, and the
truth flag. The flag separates the three populations a real scanner mixes --- the true coincidences
that carry range information, the scatter coincidences that misposition it, and the random
coincidences that add a flat background --- and the scatter counts split the scatter further into
single and multiple, the distinction a scatter correction needs. The full column schema of every
output file is generated from the code into \texttt{docs/SCHEMA.md}.

\subsection{Validation}

The controlled phantom is where the engine is checked against known answers. Transporting
\SI{511}{keV} photons through the water phantom reproduces Beer--Lambert attenuation (the unscattered
fraction $0.215$ against the analytic $0.216$). The random rate matches the analytic $2\tau S^2$ to
within the statistical uncertainty. The timing residual $\mathrm{d}t$ matches the single-photon
jitter predicted from the crystal's light yield and decay. And the full chain runs the order of
$10^{8}$ annihilations in a few minutes. Across crystals, the denser BGO contains more full-energy
events than CsI --- a higher acceptance after the photopeak cut --- while its lower light yield gives
it a coarser timing resolution, both as the physics predicts.

\section{The after-proton-irradiation (API) scenario}
\label{sec:api}

This is the scenario the simulator ultimately serves. It is outlined here; the implementation
follows.

\subsection{The idea}

A proton beam delivering a therapeutic dose fragments nuclei along its path, leaving short-lived
$\beta^+$ emitters --- $^{15}$O, $^{11}$C, and others. Their positrons annihilate nearby, so the dose
leaves a positron-activity image whose distal edge tracks the proton range. Imaging that activity with
PET, after the irradiation, and comparing the measured range to the plan is the verification this
simulator supports.

The upstream code \texttt{ptcryspg4} transports the protons and the positrons and freezes a
\emph{scenario}: the set of annihilation points --- the spatial source, with the positron range
already contained in each point --- together with, for each isotope, the number of decays measured
(the budget) for a given dose and acquisition timing.

\subsection{What changes from the clinical scenario}

Only the front of the chain changes, and in two ways. The source becomes the scenario: annihilation
points are drawn from its emitters, per isotope and to the measured budget, in place of the
concentration-weighted draw in a geometric solid. And the count becomes \emph{given} rather than
derived --- the per-isotope decay budget $N_j$ is read from the scenario, where the clinical scenario
computes $N$ from an activity. The timing generalises from one isotope to the per-isotope mixture the
scenario specifies; each emitter carries its isotope, hence its half-life. Everything downstream ---
the transport, the singles list, the true and random coincidences, the reconstruction, and the
list-mode output --- is unchanged. The machinery validated on the phantom runs without modification on
the scenario source.

\subsection{Status}

The scenario reader and the per-isotope injection are the remaining piece. The clinical scenario
provides the validated engine and chain they will feed; this section will be expanded as the scenario
format is fixed.
